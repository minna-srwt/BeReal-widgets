PMs use teardowns to:

get up to speed on a competitor's product before a strategy meeting
stress-test a design decision before shipping it
spot gaps in their own product by seeing how others solved the same problem
build a point of view before talking to users

Here's the catch: it's easy to do a teardown badly.

A weak one sounds confident but says very little.

A shallow teardown…
A strong teardown…
Judges looks:"This button should be bigger."
Explains a choice:"The button is small because the team doesn't want you to post twice."
Describes a feeling:"Onboarding is confusing."
Names a moment:"Screen 3 asks for contacts before showing what the app does."
Copies another app:"They should add a feed like Instagram."
Asks if it fits:"Would a feed break the thing this app is built around?"
A shallow teardown…Says what to change
A strong teardown…Says what you saw, why the team might have done it, what you'd test, and what would change your mind

That last row is the whole difference. A strong teardown isn't "here's what I'd fix". It's an argument you can defend, including the part where you admit what you don't know yet.

You'll build exactly that in your next task, using your own moment log.

Want to see a pro do it? Read this teardown of Superhuman's onboarding call. Superhuman is an email app known for its unusually hands-on onboarding. Notice three things:

1 Timing, not vibes.It breaks the call down minute by minute, so every claim points to an exact moment.
2 Named mechanisms, not descriptions.It doesn't say the call "felt good". It names why each part works.
3 A number, not a guess.It ties the onboarding to a business result someone would actually bet on.
