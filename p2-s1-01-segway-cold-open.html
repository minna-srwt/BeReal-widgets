In 2001, the whole tech world was obsessed with a secret.

An inventor named Dean Kamen had built something under the code name "Ginger", and he refused to say what it was.

A select few people got a private look: Steve Jobs, Jeff Bezos, a handful of investors. What they said only made it worse.

Jobs reportedly said it could be as big as the personal computer.
One investor predicted it would become the fastest company in history to hit $1 billion.

The rest of the world was left guessing.

A hydrogen-powered car? An anti-gravity machine?

On December 3, 2001, Kamen finally revealed it, live on Good Morning America, rolling across a park in New York City.

It was the Segway: a two-wheeled scooter you steer by leaning.

The tech was genuinely impressive. It balanced itself. It was safe enough that police officers and postal workers used it on the job.

DIDN'T BUY regular people

But regular people didn't buy it.

And the people who did ran into a strange problem almost immediately. They didn't know where to ride it.

In 2003, San Francisco banned Segways from its sidewalks. Out on the road, they shared lanes with cars.

Nineteen years after that big reveal, in 2020, the company stopped making the original Segway. By then it was just 1.5% of the company's revenue, and the press summed it up in one line: "popular with police but not the public."

So what went wrong?

The product worked.The hype was enormous.The money was there.

The answer isn't the technology. It's something much simpler, and you'll uncover it in this project.
